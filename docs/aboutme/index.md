# Communicate

## About Me

My name is Kelsey Skipper, and I am a mechanical engineering student at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. I am interested in the process of taking a physical problem, identifying the mechanics that govern it, and developing a practical solution that can be analyzed, tested, and improved. Mechanical engineering appeals to me because it combines mathematical modeling with physical systems: an equation can describe a component, but an engineer must still determine whether the model represents the real system and whether the resulting design satisfies its requirements. Through my coursework and engineering experiences, I am developing a foundation in mechanics, mathematics, computational problem solving, and mechanical design while learning how these subjects interact in engineering practice. I am particularly interested in design and systems that connect analytical methods with physical hardware. As I progress through my degree, I want to become an engineer who can move between analysis and implementation: someone who can identify the governing physics, make a defensible design decision, communicate the reasoning behind that decision, and evaluate whether the result satisfies the original requirements. I also recognize that engineering competence involves more than obtaining a numerical answer. An engineer must understand the assumptions behind that answer, recognize when a model is no longer appropriate, and communicate limitations honestly. This portfolio documents that development by preserving not only my final results, but also the analysis, decisions, and evidence that produced them.

### What Does It Mean to Defend an Engineering Decision; and Do I Currently Know How to Do It?

To defend an engineering decision means being able to explain not only what was chosen, but why that choice was appropriate given the requirements, constraints, available alternatives, and governing engineering principles. A defensible decision should be supported by evidence rather than preference. At the beginning of this semester, I understand the basic concept of defending a decision, but I do not yet consider myself fully proficient at doing it. I can explain the reasoning behind many of my choices, but I am still developing the ability to document that reasoning systematically through explicit criteria, quantitative comparisons, assumptions, and supporting evidence. My goal for this semester is to make my engineering decisions increasingly reproducible: another engineer should be able to examine my work and understand the reasoning well enough to agree with the decision, identify a limitation, or propose a better alternative.
